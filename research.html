Research & Writing

Embodied epistemology & contemplative phenomenology.

My work in philosophy of mind and cognitive science asks how gut feelings function as knowledge: what they represent, how the body changes their epistemic status, when they warrant trust, and what it takes to read them well. My contemplative science work examines the nature of bodily phenomenology in Jewish contemplative practice and in advanced Buddhist contemplative practice. I combine conceptual and phenomenological analysis with findings from empirical research, particularly through the Meditation Research Program at Harvard Medical School/Massachusetts General Hospital.

Gut feelings & embodied knowing

My dissertation, "Gut Feelings and Embodied Epistemology," examines a neglected subtype of intuition: intuition in the bodily mode. I argue that intuitions have modes of presentation—the same content can become manifest intellectually, perceptually, or viscerally—and that gut feelings carry valenced, evaluative, and intentional content whose bodily character is constitutive rather than incidental. Because gut feelings present in the body, they raise epistemic problems distinct from those facing intuition in the intellectual mode: when does a bodily feeling give us reason to believe something, and how do we distinguish an epistemically useful bodily response from anxiety or prejudice? I develop embodied epistemology as a framework for using and justifying gut feelings: by coming into a felt, inferential, and critical relationship with the experiences, habits, and histories likely to have shaped one's intuitions within a domain, one becomes more justified in relying on them. The account is enactive, rooted in feminist epistemology and the 4E cognition literature, arguing that intuition is a kind of skillful perception and that a body's gendered, relational, and cultural history shapes intuition's outputs.

"Are Gut Feelings Mere Emotion?"

Under review · Presentation at the 2027 APA Central Division

Against the objection that gut feelings reduce to embodied appraisals, I argue that gut feelings bear a distinctive epistemic signature: an essential incongruity between the valence one experiences toward the object of the intuition and the valence one would expect given everything else one knows. One consequence is that gut feelings can be phenomenologically identical to certain cases of anxiety—a feature I argue is a virtue of the theory rather than a problem, since an adequate account of intuition should predict the phenomenon that makes gut feelings epistemically disorienting.

Bodily awareness in advanced meditation

"Bodily Awareness in Advanced Meditation: A Three-Level Model of Interoceptive, Schematic, and Pacificatory Changes"

R&R at Mindfulness · With Terje Sparby and Matthew D. Sacchet · Preprint

Existing approaches to bodily awareness in advanced meditation either subsume its changes under frameworks of self-dissolution or operationalize them through interoceptive measures that assume ongoing bodily sensation. We propose a three-level model distinguishing interoceptive changes, body-schematic changes, and pacification—the complete phenomenological absence of bodily awareness—by drawing on jhāna practice manuals and neuroimaging studies of advanced concentrative absorption. The model generates testable predictions about the partial dissociability of these dimensions and bears on wider debates about body representation, interoception, and the structure of consciousness.

"The Content and Structure of Dream Reports Are Coupled to Affect"

Forthcoming in Scientific Reports (Nature) · With Luke Leckie, Anya K. Bershad, Mason McClay, Sofiia Rappe, and Jacob G. Foster

We combine word embedding, topic modeling, and network analysis on over 18,000 dream reports to show that the affective content of dreams is systematically tied to their thematic structure. Positively valenced reports exhibit semantically coherent connections between successive topics; negatively valenced reports focus on structurally dominant topics with less coherent connections.

Jewish contemplative practice

Devekut and seamless practice

"Devekut and the Relational Structure of Advanced Meditation"

In progress · With the Meditation Research Program

I argue that contemplative practices underrepresented in the science present conceptual dimensions of phenomenology that existing theories of advanced meditation have not adequately captured: devekut's entry points, including prayer, wordless melody, and everyday activity, are not classically contemplative; its pathway to advanced absorption is relational rather than detachment-based; and its training often occurs across domains of life rather than within formal practice.

"Seamless Practice in Jewish Contemplative Life"

Solo-authored · In progress

A phenomenological account of devekut and seamless practice: the sliding scale from intense absorption to a constant background awareness of divinity, and the philosophical question of what licenses calling points along that scale the same practice.

My research on devekut and Jewish meditation is grounded in my three-year teacher training in core Hasidic and Kabbalistic texts through Or HaLev and the IJS Gates of Awareness program, which seeded my engagement with these sources and allows me to bring a philosophy of mind background to a textual tradition rarely read alongside cognitive science.

When the Body Knows

When the Body Knows develops my dissertation into a monograph on how we ought to proceed when it seems that the body "knows" something different than we actively believe. The book argues that bodily awareness is a trainable epistemic skill: everyday gut feelings and advanced contemplative states are different stages in cultivating the same capacity. It braids an account of gut feelings—intuitions known through the body rather than the intellect, whose trustworthiness hinges on the histories that shaped them—with the study of contemplative traditions that deliberately train bodily awareness. Drawing on philosophy of mind, contemplative science, and Jewish and Buddhist contemplative practice, I treat these traditions as lineages that cultivate the skill of bodily knowing and carry their own epistemologies of trust and interpretation. The book asks how we can learn from our body's felt responses without assuming that what feels compelling is necessarily true.

I develop the argument comparatively, through two contemplative traditions that train bodily attunement while embedding it in a way of life: Theravāda Buddhist practice, on which contemplative science has built nearly all of its models, and Jewish contemplative practice, which it has largely ignored. The book is also clear-sighted about the risks of embedded practice, contending that popular literature on the body, including trauma somatics and intuition science, glosses over the ways embodied knowing is particular and situated—my gut feeling does not mean what yours means, because we do not have the same history.
